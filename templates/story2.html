When Every Night Felt Like a Risk

Mr. Ramesh Patel, 68, was admitted to City General Hospital's Neurology ICU in April 2024 following a mild ischaemic stroke. While his prognosis was optimistic — the stroke had been caught early and treated within the golden hour — the recovery phase presented its own set of challenges. Post-stroke patients frequently experience episodes of dizziness, spatial disorientation, and sudden weakness in affected limbs, making unsupported movement during the night hours particularly dangerous.

His son Rohan, who lives in Bangalore and could not be present full-time, described the first few days as "a constant low-level anxiety." "Dad has always been stubborn about asking for help," he explained. "We knew he'd try to get up on his own in the middle of the night. That was what worried us most."

The Privacy Question

When the ICU nursing team informed the Patel family that FallGuard AI monitoring would be active in Mr. Patel's room, Rohan's first reaction was hesitation. His father, a retired schoolteacher with a strong sense of personal dignity, had always been private. The idea of video surveillance — even in a clinical setting — felt intrusive.

The nursing team arranged a brief explanation of exactly what FallGuard does and does not capture. Using the dashboard's Privacy Mode as a demonstration, they showed the family the skeletal wireframe view that monitoring staff actually see: a black canvas, cyan joint markers, and no identifiable features whatsoever.

"Once they showed us what it actually looked like," Rohan said, "my dad actually laughed. He said it looked like a video game. He didn't mind at all after that."

The Alert That Mattered Most

On the ninth day of admission, at 3:44 AM, Mr. Patel attempted to stand unaided to use the bathroom — something his physiotherapist had specifically advised against without supervision. FallGuard's model detected the rising motion, the shift in centre of gravity, and the micro-tremor pattern in his left-side landmarks consistent with post-stroke weakness under load.

The confidence score hit 88.7% across the required confirmation window. The alert fired. The on-call ICU nurse reached Mr. Patel before he had fully stood, providing the support that prevented what could have been a severe secondary fall.

ICU Nurse — Night Shift

"The alert included the room number and confidence level. I was there in under a minute. Mr. Patel was surprised to see me — he hadn't pressed any button. When I explained the system had detected him, he went very quiet, then said: 'So someone was watching out for me all along.' That stayed with me."

Recovery and Discharge

Mr. Patel was discharged after 14 days with a positive rehabilitation prognosis. His neurologist noted that the absence of secondary falls during recovery was a significant factor in his smooth rehabilitation trajectory. Falls during the acute post-stroke window can reset neurological recovery progress substantially.

For the Patel family, FallGuard changed how they thought about hospital monitoring entirely. Rohan wrote to the hospital trust after discharge: "Before this, I thought surveillance meant someone watching my father like he was a subject. What FallGuard did was the opposite — it watched over him, the way family would if we could all be there at 3 AM. It protected his dignity at the same time. That combination is something I didn't know was possible."

Why This Story Matters for Neurology Patients

Post-stroke patients represent one of the highest-risk populations for in-hospital falls. Neurological deficits affecting balance, proprioception, and spatial awareness make unsupported ambulation acutely dangerous, particularly in the first two weeks post-event. Yet this is also the population most likely to attempt independent movement — driven by the same sense of autonomy that characterised their pre-stroke lives.

FallGuard's continuous monitoring closes this gap without requiring behavioural change from the patient. It does not ask Mr. Patel to press a button, call out, or wait. It simply watches — invisibly, respectfully, and with precision — and acts when the data says the risk is real.
